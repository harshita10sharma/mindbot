# Thinking traps (cognitive distortions)

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) describes common "thinking traps": automatic thoughts that feel true but are distorted. Everyone has them. Noticing them is the first step to feeling better.

All-or-nothing thinking: seeing things in black and white. "If I don't get full marks, I'm a failure."
Overgeneralisation: treating one event as a never-ending pattern, often with words like "always" and "never". "Nobody ever listens to me."
Mental filter: focusing only on the negative and ignoring the positive.
Disqualifying the positive: "That compliment doesn't count, they were just being nice."
Mind reading: assuming you know what others think. "They think I'm stupid."
Fortune telling: predicting things will go badly without evidence.
Catastrophising: expecting the worst possible outcome. "If I fail this test my life is over."
Emotional reasoning: "I feel worthless, so I must be worthless."
Should statements: rigid rules such as "I should never make mistakes" or "People should always respect me."
Labelling: attaching a global label to yourself or others, like "I'm a loser" or "They're all idiots."
Personalisation: blaming yourself for things outside your control.
Blaming: placing all responsibility on other people and none on yourself.

How to challenge a thinking trap:
1. Write down the thought exactly.
2. Ask: what is the evidence for it, and against it?
3. Ask: is there another way to see this situation?
4. Ask: what would I say to a friend who had this thought?
5. Write a more balanced thought, and notice how your feeling changes.

This method is called a thought record and is one of the most researched tools in CBT.
